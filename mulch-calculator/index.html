How deep should mulch be?

For most planting beds the target is 2 to 3 inches. Thinner than 2 inches and mulch stops doing its two main jobs — it won't block enough light to suppress weed seeds, and it won't meaningfully slow evaporation from the soil. Thicker than 4 inches and you start causing problems: water struggles to reach the root zone, the bottom layer can turn anaerobic and sour, and you've built good cover for voles.

Depth means finished depth, not how much you add. If you're refreshing a bed that already holds an inch and a half of settled mulch, you only need an inch or so on top to get back to 3. Poke a finger in and measure what's there first — over-buying on the refresh year is the most common mistake.

Whatever the depth, keep mulch off trunks and stems. Pull it back 3 to 6 inches from tree trunks and away from perennial crowns. Mulch piled against bark holds moisture against it and invites rot.

2 inches or 3?

Choose 2 inches for fine-textured mulches — shredded hardwood, fine compost, cocoa hulls — which knit together and block light at a shallower depth. Two inches is also right when you're top-dressing an established bed, working around shallow-rooted shrubs like azaleas and rhododendrons, or filling tight spots where a deeper layer would bury stems.

Choose 3 inches for chunky material like pine bark nuggets, which has large air gaps and settles noticeably in the first month. Three is also the call for new beds over bare soil, beds with heavy weed pressure, and full-sun areas that dry out fast. Save 4 inches for paths and utility areas.

Why the bag count rounds up

Three reasons. Bags are sold whole. The volume printed on the bag is loose fill measured before it compresses — in the bag, and again under its own weight once it's spread. And real beds aren't clean rectangles; curves, edges, and low spots quietly eat more than the arithmetic predicts. Coming up 10% short at the far end of the bed on a Sunday afternoon costs a lot more than one leftover bag.

For reference, a cubic yard is 13.5 bags at 2 cu ft, or 9 bags at 3 cu ft. Once you're past roughly 2 cubic yards, bulk delivery is usually cheaper per yard than bagged — if you have somewhere to dump it.
